HOW THE FILE IS ORGANISED
  Section 1  Index by exam: every written question, in paper order, with the topic code
             that holds its answer. Section 1B: the oral-exam question sets, same idea,
             plus what the examiner reportedly wanted to hear.
  Section 2  Answers grouped by topic (A fundamentals/evaluation, B fingerprint, C iris,
             D face, E multibiometrics, F topics asked only in the oral exam / notes,
             G normalisation). Repeated questions are answered once; the wording of each
             variant and the exams it appeared in are listed on top, and the answer covers
             every sub-question of every variant. "O12.3" = oral set 12, question 3.
  Section 3  Where the items of "Exams SB.txt" are answered.
  Section 4  Numbers to memorise.
  Section 5  Mistakes found in the student solved sheet (do not copy those).
  Section 6  Open points / what I could not verify.

TAGS USED IN THE ANSWERS
  [S p.N]  = taken from the slides, PDF page N of IBB_slides_Jan25.pdf
  [X]      = standard textbook knowledge that is NOT on the slides (safe extra, for depth)
  [W]      = checked against an outside source on the web (listed in section 6)
  [!]      = the solved sheet says something different or wrong here
  [?]      = I could not pin the expected answer down; reasoning given

ABOUT "100 % OF THE POINTS"
  I do not have the examiner's marking key, so no one can promise full marks. What this
  file does: every point the slides make about a question is in the answer; where the
  slides are thin, the missing part is filled from standard literature and marked [X].
  In an exam, answer in short bullet points, add a sketch when the question says
  "sketch/draw/illustrate", and quote the numbers (they are cheap points).

ABBREVIATIONS
  FMR/FAR false match / false accept rate     FNMR/FRR false non-match / false reject rate
  GMR genuine match rate (= 1 - FNMR)         EER equal error rate     tau decision threshold
  FTE/FTA failure to enroll / to acquire      HD Hamming distance      IPD inter-pupillary distance
  SP singular point    L1/L2/L3 feature levels    VJ Viola-Jones    NIR near-infrared
  ppi pixels per inch  PCA/LDA principal component / linear discriminant analysis

A1  Scientific facts about biometrics; suitability of a trait
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ASKED IN: 2018 Q1 (which statements are scientific facts) ; 2017 Q5 (factors for the
          suitability of a trait)

ANSWER 2018 Q1  (a) NOT a fact   (b) NOT a fact   (c) FACT   (d) FACT   (e) FACT
  (a) Uniqueness: the physical trait itself may not be unique, and uniqueness of the
      modalities has not been scientifically clearly established. Evidence on the slides:
      "Only once during the existence of our solar system will two human beings be born
      with similar finger markings" (Harper's, 1910); "two like fingerprints would be
      found only once every 10^48 years" (Scientific American, 1911) - claims without a
      scientific proof. [S p.42]
  (b) Permanence: also not an established scientific fact. [S p.42]
  (c) Fact 1: biometric systems rely only on the digital measurements (a sensor sample ->
      features), never on the physical trait itself. [S p.43]
  (d) Fact 2: sensing introduces variations in the samples of the same trait of a user
      obtained over time (pose, pressure, illumination, sensor noise, ...). [S p.43, 44]
  (e) Fact 3: feature sets from different samples of the same trait are seldom identical
      -> we look for a close, not a perfect, match. So two IDENTICAL feature sets are
      suspicious: probably a replay of recorded data by an adversary. [S p.43]

ANSWER 2017 Q5  Seven factors for the suitability of a physical/behavioural trait [X]
  (Jain, Ross, Prabhakar 2004. The list is not on these slides; the solved sheet has it.)
  1 Universality     everybody should possess the trait -> minimises failure to enroll (FTE)
  2 Uniqueness       different persons have different traits -> FMR must stay low enough
  3 Permanence       trait invariant over time -> otherwise high FNMR (template aging)
  4 Measurability    (also "collectability") can be acquired and digitised with a sensor
  5 Performance      accuracy, speed and robustness reachable (samples processed per time)
  6 Acceptability    people are willing to present the trait (cultural/privacy issues)
  7 Circumvention    how easily the trait can be imitated/spoofed to evade recognition

A2  Authentication types and recognition types; probe and gallery
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ASKED IN: 2017 Q1 ; 2023 Q1

ANSWER  [S p.20-22]
 AUTHENTICATION TYPES (the TASK the system solves - what question it answers)
  - Pair matching ......... Do these two images match?
  - Verification (1:1) .... Does this sample of subject X match the template of X in our
                            system? (claimed identity, one comparison)
  - Identification (1:n) .. Does this person exist in our system / who is it? (compare to
                            the whole gallery, no claimed identity)
  - Negative authentication I am NOT subject X / not a member of group X.
  - Deduplication ......... Do we have to enroll you? (is this person already enrolled?)
 RECOGNITION TYPES (the STATUS of the subject towards the system)
  - Positive recognition .. the sample represents a subject KNOWN to the system (already
                            registered) - e.g. access control, verification, identification.
  - Negative recognition .. the sample represents a subject UNKNOWN to the system (not yet
                            registered) - e.g. watch-list search (US-VISIT: does the visitor
                            have multiple aliases?), de-duplication (UID India: is the same
                            person acquiring a second 12-digit number?). [S p.52-53]
 DIFFERENCE (2023 Q1): authentication types are different operating modes / questions
  (pair matching, 1:1, 1:n, negative, dedup); recognition types only say whether the
  subject is expected to be in the database (positive) or not (negative). Verification
  and identification normally serve positive recognition; negative authentication and
  deduplication serve negative recognition.
 PROBE = a sample presented to the biometric system. GALLERY = the collection of enrolled
  templates. [S p.22]

A3  Enrolment vs recognition pipeline
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ASKED IN: 2025 Q1 ; notes N10, N29

ANSWER  [S p.23-28]
   ENROLMENT:     new user -> Acquisition -> Feature extraction -> Template generation
                  (-> template stored in the gallery/database)
   RECOGNITION:   decision needed -> Acquisition -> Feature extraction -> Matching
                  (probe vs template(s): 1:1 or 1:n) -> Labeling/decision (threshold tau)
 DIFFERENCES
  - Enrolment ENDS with a stored template; it makes no decision. Recognition has two extra
    stages: matching (similarity/distance score) and labeling (accept/reject or identity).
  - Enrolment is done once, usually supervised, with quality control, several measurements,
    selection of the best samples and merging of samples (user's view, p.25). Recognition
    happens at every access and has to be fast (speed vs security trade-off, operational
    threshold, number of attempts, auditing - security engineer's view, p.28).
  - Enrolment questions: is meta-data stored with the record, multiple templates per user,
    operator supervision, how is the template stored (encryption, template protection).
    Recognition questions: how is the template retrieved (on device / local server / cloud).
    [S p.26, 28]
  - Same first two steps (acquisition, feature extraction) in both; the template of the
    enrolment is what the recognition compares against.

A5  Match-score distributions (ideal vs real), similarity vs distance score, threshold
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ASKED IN: 2025 Q2 ; 2017 Q2 ; notes N24 ("... what appears because of it")

ANSWER  [S p.31-39]
 SCORES: similarity score - higher is better (match if s >= tau); distance score - lower
  is better (0 for a self match; match if d <= tau). Both are produced in the MATCHING
  stage of the recognition pipeline and compared with the decision threshold tau.
  Note [S p.43]: a distance of exactly 0 against a stored template is suspicious (samples
  of one trait are seldom identical -> possible replay).
 IDEAL WORLD: the distribution of scores for different persons (d = impostor) and for the
  same person (g = genuine) are WELL SEPARATED (no overlap). A threshold exists that gives
  FMR = 0 and FNMR = 0 at the same time.
                 frequency
        d ____             ____ g
         /    \           /    \          <- no overlap, tau in the gap
        /      \         /      \
   ----/--------\-------/--------\---- similarity
                 tau
 REAL WORLD: the two distributions OVERLAP (their tails cross; real histograms are not
  even Gaussian - see the irregular genuine curve on p.39).
                 frequency
          d ____        ____ g
           /    \  tau /    \
          /      \ |  /      \     blue = false non-match (genuine below tau)
   ------/--------\|/--------\--   red  = false match (impostor above tau)
 PROBLEM / WHAT APPEARS BECAUSE OF IT: the decision threshold. Errors appear - false
  matches (type I) and false non-matches (type II). The FAR and the FRR CANNOT be reduced
  simultaneously by adjusting the threshold: raising tau lowers FMR but raises FNMR and
  vice versa. So the operating point is a compromise chosen by the application (high
  security -> low FMR; Disney World ticketing -> low FNMR [S p.112]); this trade-off is
  what the ROC/DET curves and the EER describe (see A7). Extra error sources outside the
  matcher: failure to acquire / failure to enroll. [S p.40-41]

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
A6  FMR, FNMR, GMR; how FNMR is calculated; problems with a high FNMR
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ASKED IN: 2017 Q3 ; 2018 Q2 ; notes N23 (GMR GNMR - FNMR FMR), N24

ANSWER  [S p.29-39]
 DEFINITIONS (similarity scores s, threshold tau)
  - False Match (type I): an impostor sample matched a reference template.
  - False Non-Match (type II): a genuine sample did not match its reference template.
  - FNMR(tau) = P(s < tau | genuine)  = (# genuine comparisons with score < tau) / (# all
        genuine comparisons)  = integral of the genuine density from -inf to tau.
  - FMR(tau)  = P(s >= tau | impostor) = (# impostor comparisons with score >= tau) / (# all
        impostor comparisons) = integral of the impostor density from tau to +inf.
  - GMR = 1 - FNMR (genuine match rate = true accept rate). "GNMR" is not a standard
    abbreviation; read it as the genuine non-match rate = FNMR [?]. Correspondingly the
    impostor non-match rate = 1 - FMR. FAR/FRR are the system-level synonyms of FMR/FNMR
    (they additionally count failures to acquire).
 "If we know the densities we integrate them, but typically we do not, so we calculate the
  rates with a benchmark data set" - i.e. run many genuine and impostor comparisons and
  count. [S p.35-36]
 SKETCH: two bell curves (d left, g right), vertical line tau; the area of g to the LEFT
  of tau is the FNMR; the area of d to the RIGHT of tau is the FMR.
 BEHAVIOUR AS A FUNCTION OF tau [S p.38]: FMR falls from ~100 % to 0 as tau grows, FNMR
  rises from 0 to ~100 %; they cross at the EER; "FNMR @ zero FMR" and "FMR @ zero FNMR"
  are the end points.
 2018 Q2 - PROBLEMS PERTAINING TO A HIGH FNMR
  - Causes: intra-user variations (pose, illumination, pressure, dry/wet finger, partial
    prints, defocus, occlusion), sensor noise, change of sensor, template aging, pupil
    dilation, failure to acquire. [S p.44, 112, 129, 115]
  - Consequences: genuine users are rejected -> inconvenience, repeated attempts, manual
    fallback and cost, loss of user acceptance. It is a problem above all when the
    THROUGHPUT is large: e.g. a 1:1 verification kiosk at an airport with 5000 people per
    hour for 14 h/day = 70 000 verifications a day; even a FNMR of 1 % means ~700 falsely
    rejected people a day (~50 an hour) -> queues. FNMR cannot just be lowered with a
    looser threshold because FMR (security) then rises (A5). Direct comparison of error
    rates is valid only on the same database/sensor/environment/application. [S p.112]

A7  ROC, DET, EER, AUC, CMC; comparing systems; which graphical metric to use
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ASKED IN: 2018 Q3 ; 2017 Q4 ; 2022-02 Q9 ; 2024-01 Q1 ; 2024-08 Q9 ; 2025 Q7

ANSWER
 ROC (Receiver Operating Characteristic) [S p.191, 193]: plots the genuine match rate
  (1 - FNMR, TPR) against the false match rate (FPR) over ALL thresholds (the slide also
  shows the FNMR-vs-FMR form). It describes the whole trade-off of a system and removes the
  dependence on one chosen threshold, so two systems that use different thresholds/score
  ranges become comparable. Curve closer to the upper-left corner = better.
 DET (Detection Error Tradeoff) [S p.192]: FNMR (FRR) against FMR (FAR), usually on log (or
  normal-deviate) axes; curve closer to the bottom-left corner = better; shows the low-error
  region much more clearly than a linear ROC.
 HOW TO COMPARE TWO SYSTEMS (2018 Q3, 2025 Q7) [S p.193]: the best way is to examine their
  ROC curves. If the FRR of system A is consistently lower than the FRR of system B at the
  corresponding values of FAR, then the matching performance of A is better than B.
 EER = the point of the ROC/DET curve (threshold) where FMR = FNMR (FAR = FRR); lower EER =
  better; a single number, but it describes only one operating point. Safest way to
  state where it is read: on the plot of the error rates against the threshold (slide p.38)
  it is the crossing of the FMR and FNMR curves; on a DET curve it is where the curve meets
  the diagonal FNMR = FMR. [?] One oral-exam report (O24.4): a student who said "EER is read
  off the DET curve where the two errors are equal" was told "no" by the examiner - the
  reason is not recorded, so give the error-vs-threshold description above.
 AUC (area under the ROC curve) [S p.194]: one number for the whole curve, "use when you
  can't pick a threshold". Range 0.5 to 1 (slide scale: 0.9-1.0 excellent, 0.8-0.9 good,
  0.7-0.8 fair, 0.6-0.7 poor, 0.5-0.6 fail). AUC = 0.5 means the curve lies on the diagonal:
  the system has NO discriminative power - it ranks genuine vs impostor like a coin toss
  (chance level) [W]. [!] The solved sheet calls it the "worst case"; strictly an AUC below
  0.5 would be worse than chance (inverted scores), 0.5 is chance level.
 THE TWO CURVES THAT INTERSECT (2022-02 Q9)
  - Properties of the curves FMR(tau), FNMR(tau): monotone, opposite directions; FMR(tau)
    non-increasing, FNMR(tau) non-decreasing in the similarity threshold; they cross at the
    EER; neither can reach zero without the other going to its maximum (A5).
  - Two ROC/DET curves crossing at one point: neither system dominates; each is better in a
    different operating region. Compare at the operating point the application needs:
    high-security application (low FMR) -> choose the system with the lower FNMR in the
    low-FMR part of the DET (left of the crossing); convenience application (low FNMR
    tolerated, higher FMR) -> choose the system with the lower FMR in the low-FNMR part
    (right of the crossing). If no operating point is prescribed use AUC / EER as a
    tie-break, or report FNMR at a fixed FMR (e.g. FNMR @ FMR = 0.1 %, as in the NIST iris
    challenge). [S p.179, 182, 193]
 WHICH METRIC FOR WHICH SYSTEM (2024-01 Q1, 2024-08 Q9) [S p.56]
  - VERIFICATION (1:1): FMR, FNMR, EER, ROC/DET curve (graphical), AUC.
    Graphical = ROC or DET: it shows the error trade-off over all thresholds.
  - IDENTIFICATION (1:n): rank-1 identification rate (recognition rate), rank-k rate,
    precision, recall; graphical = CMC (Cumulative Match Characteristic): fraction of
    probes whose true identity is within the top-k of the ranked list, plotted against k
    [X]. Used because identification returns a RANKED list, not a yes/no. (Oral O8.5: "CMC,
    not ROC" - ROC is the wrong curve for identification; he also asked for the AUC of the
    CMC, i.e. the area under the CMC curve as a single number.)
  - DETECTION / SEGMENTATION: precision, recall, F1, IoU/Dice; graphical = precision-recall
    curve (or ROC with number of false positives, as in the FDDB face-detection
    benchmark [S p.248]) [X]. Preferred over accuracy because the classes are heavily
    imbalanced (A8).

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
A8  What is wrong with accuracy as a detection metric
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ASKED IN: 2023 Q2

ANSWER  [X - the principle; the slides only mention sliding-window detection and FDDB]
 Accuracy = (TP + TN) / (TP + TN + FP + FN). In detection with a sliding window almost
 all windows are negatives, so the classes are extremely imbalanced and accuracy is
 dominated by the majority class.
 EXAMPLE: an image with 11 faces and 1000 non-face windows. A "detector" that always
 answers "no face" is correct on 1000 of 1011 windows = 98.9 % (~99 %) accurate, yet it
 finds 0 faces (recall 0). A real detector that finds 10 of the 11 faces but also
 raises 20 false alarms has TP = 10, FP = 20, FN = 1, TN = 980 -> accuracy 97.9 %, LOWER
 than the useless detector. [!] (the solved sheet's numbers for this example are garbled)
 MORE SUITABLE: precision = TP / (TP + FP) (how many detections are real faces), recall =
 TP / (TP + FN) (how many faces were found), F1 = 2PR / (P + R); or the precision-recall /
 ROC curve over the detector threshold. They ignore the huge TN count.

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
A9  Cross-validation; validation protocols
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ASKED IN: 2022-01 Q6 ; notes N22 (cross-validation), N25 (validation protocols)

ANSWER  [S p.56, 200 ; X]
 CROSS-VALIDATION (k-fold):
  1. Split the labelled data set of N samples into k disjoint folds of about N/k samples.
  2. For each fold i = 1..k: train the model on the other k-1 folds, test (validate) it on
     fold i. (k training sessions, each on N - N/k samples.)
  3. Average the k results and report mean and standard deviation (slide: "5-fold cross
     validation: means and standard deviations").
  Example: 10-fold -> 10 trainings, each with 90 % of the data, each sample is used for
  testing exactly once.
  LEAVE-ONE-OUT (oral O14.4) = the extreme case k = N: N trainings, each on N-1 samples,
  tested on the single left-out sample (for biometrics: leave one SUBJECT out). Nearly
  unbiased and uses the most training data, but the most expensive and with a high-variance
  estimate [X].
 WHY: it estimates how the trained model GENERALISES to unseen data (detects overfitting),
  is far more reliable than a single train/test split because every sample is used for
  both training and testing, gives an error bar, and is used to tune hyper-parameters.
 BIOMETRIC NUANCE: the folds must be PERSON-DISJOINT (all samples of one subject in the
  same fold); otherwise the same person is in training and testing and the result is
  too optimistic. The slides use "person-disjoint 10-fold cross-validation" for contact-lens
  detection. [S p.200]
 VALIDATION PROTOCOLS (why they matter): needed for sound comparisons of techniques and for
  repeatability. A protocol defines the procedure, metrics, databases and frameworks. The
  slides' example (AWE database + toolbox) [S p.56]: partition the data into a development
  set (60 % of the images) and a test set (40 %); 5-fold cross-validation (means and
  standard deviations); bootstrapping on the test set (means and standard deviations);
  files with the lists for the experiments are distributed with the dataset; metrics: ROC
  and EER for verification, CMC and Rank-1 for identification.

 FINGERMARKS/latents, which contain far fewer minutiae (avg ~18, NIST SD27: 5..82) than
 rolled or plain prints, and as extra evidence for good prints; why = with so few L2
 features the L2 evidence alone is too weak, so L3 adds discriminative information. Needs
 1000 ppi. Open problem: how to reliably ENCODE and MATCH L3 features in poor-quality
 fingermarks (state-of-the-art extractors fail -> semi-manual extraction). [S p.77-78, 126-128]

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
B2  Singular points, pattern types, number of singular points; linking the terms
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ASKED IN: 2022-01 Q3 ; 2017 Q6 ; 2018 Q5 ; 2020 Q7 ; 2024-08 Q1 (SP part)

ANSWER  [S p.64-68, 94]
 SINGULAR POINTS: salient locations where the ridge orientations change abruptly. Two
  basic types: LOOP (the core is the north-most loop-type SP, used as a landmark to align
  the print) and DELTA (triangular ridge meeting point). If a print has no SP the "core"
  is the point of maximum ridge curvature.
 SPs are an abstract representation of the orientation map (the map can be roughly
  predicted from number and location of SPs); an even better abstraction is the PATTERN
  TYPE (fingerprint class), deduced from the NUMBER of loops and deltas and the SPATIAL
  RELATIONSHIP between them.
 PATTERN TYPES, number of singular points, and frequency on the slide
    0 SP ...... plain arch                                         (4 %)
    2 SP ...... 1 loop + 1 delta:  tented arch (3 %), left loop, right loop
                (left + right loops together 65 %)
    4 SP ...... 2 loops + 2 deltas: whorl (24 %), twin loop (4 %)
   (PCASYS classifies into six classes: arch, left loop, right loop, tented arch, whorl,
    scar [S p.67].)
 The types that depend on the spatial relationship of ONE loop and ONE delta: tented arch
  (loop and delta lie almost vertically above each other), left loop, right loop (delta
  on the side opposite to the loop opening). Types with TWO loops and TWO deltas: whorl
  (the two cores close together/spiral) and twin loop (two separate loops side by side).
 2017 Q6: majority of prints = LOOPS (65 % on the slide; the left loop is the most frequent
  single class [X]). Expected number of SPs in a fingerprint: 0, 2 or 4.
 2022-01 Q3 (ROLLED prints): on rolled prints we can observe ALL pattern types, because a
  rolled print is a complete impression (nail to nail), so all singular points are visible
  (classification "requires the fingerprint to be complete, i.e. a good-quality rolled
  fingerprint"). Plain impressions/fingermarks are partial, so their class is treated as
  unknown and fingermarks are searched against all types. From the number of SPs:
  0 -> plain arch; 2 -> tented arch OR left OR right loop; 4 -> whorl OR twin loop; the
  spatial relationship then separates the classes inside each group.
 2020 Q7 - LINKING singular points, pattern type, minutiae, resolution: L1 features = the
  singular points; they are an abstract representation of the orientation field, and the
  pattern type is an even more abstract description built from the number/arrangement of
  loops and deltas. Both are visible in LOW-resolution images (250 ppi). With a higher
  resolution sensor (500 ppi) we can analyse L2 features, the minutiae, which carry much
  more discriminative information and are used for matching; at 1000 ppi also L3 (pores,
  contours). So resolution decides which level of the hierarchy is observable.

B3  Tenprint card

ANSWER  [S p.58-60, 71]
 A tenprint card holds the fingerprints of all ten fingers:
  - TOP: two rows of five ROLLED impressions (nail to nail). Row 1 = right hand, thumb to
    little finger (1 thumb, 2 index, 3 middle, 4 ring, 5 little); row 2 = left hand, thumb

B4  Fingerprint sensors (FTIR in detail, ultrasound in detail)
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ASKED IN: 2018 Q6 ; 2022-02 Q2 ; note N1

ANSWER  [S p.79-84]  - five live-scan technologies (+ swipe sensors)
  1 Optical FTIR-based sensor
  2 Capacitance-based sensor
  3 Ultrasound reflection-based sensor
  4 Piezoelectric effect-based sensor
  5 Temperature differential-based sensor

 OPTICAL FTIR (Frustrated Total Internal Reflection) - sketch and image formation [S p.79]
          LED (light source)
              \  (light enters the prism at a defined angle)
        +------\--------------------+
        |  glass | prism / platen    |   <- finger touches the top surface
        |   valley   ridge  valley   |
        |     \       /\      /      |
        |      \_____/  \____/       |
        +--------\-----------\-------+
        total internal       ridge touching glass: total internal
        reflection at a      reflection is FRUSTRATED -> light is
        valley (air gap)     absorbed / randomly scattered
              |
              v  reflected light --> lens --> CCD/CMOS camera --> image
  How the image is made: the finger is placed on the glass platen. The LED light hits the
  glass-air boundary at an angle above the critical angle. Under a VALLEY there is air, so
  the light is totally internally reflected and travels on to the lens and camera. Where a
  RIDGE touches the glass, the skin couples the light out (total internal reflection is
  "frustrated"): the light is absorbed/randomly scattered and little reaches the camera. The
  camera therefore records a gray-scale image with dark ridges on a bright background of
  valleys [W: ridges dark, valleys bright - the optics source confirms the mechanism; the
  "dark ridges" consequence is an inference].
  Disadvantages: difficult to have this arrangement in a compact form (the focal length of
  small lenses can be very large); image distortions are possible when the reflected light is
  not focused properly. [S p.79]

 CAPACITANCE-BASED [S p.80]: the most commonly used; very small in size; an array of
  electrodes; the fingerprint skin is the other electrode of a miniature capacitor; the
  charge depends on the distance to the plate, so the capacitance under a ridge is higher
  than under a valley; proper grounding is needed because of electrostatic discharges.

 ULTRASOUND REFLECTION-BASED [S p.81]: sends acoustic signals toward the fingertip and
  captures the echo signal; from the echoes it computes a RANGE image. Acoustic energy is
  reflected at every change of acoustic impedance: at a ridge in contact with the platen
  and at a valley (air gap) the echo differs in strength and time of flight, so ridge and
  valley positions are recovered. It images the SUBSURFACE of the fingertip, too. Pros:
  resilient to dirt and oil accumulations. Cons: expensive.

 PIEZOELECTRIC [S p.82]: pressure-sensitive; a mechanical stress produces a small
  electrical current (piezoelectric effect); does not capture the structure accurately
  because of its low sensitivity.
 TEMPERATURE DIFFERENTIAL [S p.83]: pyro-electric material producing a current from
  temperature differences (lasts ~100 ms); sensor kept hot by electrical heating; only
  4-bit gray scale (Atmel 2005).
 SWIPE sensors [S p.84]: software stitching of the strips; may produce artifacts -> not
  acceptable for forensic quality; cheaper (consumer electronics).

B5  Fingerprint feature extraction at levels 1 and 2 (sequence of steps)
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ASKED IN: 2023 Q4 ; (orientation-field details for B6)

ANSWER  [S p.85-100]  Commercial systems use L1 and L2 features.
   fingerprint image
        |
        v
   (1) ridge ORIENTATION and FREQUENCY estimation (per local block)
       - model the local ridge pattern as a cosine wave (amplitude, frequency, orientation);
         take its 2D FFT; the maximum of the magnitude spectrum gives the wave parameters
       - ridges are not exact cosines -> the FT gives a pair of blurred impulses, so the
         magnitude spectrum is first smoothed with a low-pass filter and then the maximum
         is detected
       - orientation is defined mod pi, so averaging 1 deg and 179 deg must give 0 deg, not
         90 deg -> smooth with the vector field V = (cos 2theta, sin 2theta); creases are
         suppressed by the smoothing
        |
        +--------> L1: (2) SINGULARITY extraction (Poincare index) -> loops, deltas, core
        |              (+ direction of singularity) -> (3) PATTERN-TYPE classification
        |
        v   L2:
   (4) ENHANCEMENT (filtering the ridges with the estimated orientation/frequency)
   (5) BINARIZATION: global threshold (Otsu) or locally computed thresholds
   (6) THINNING (morphological) -> ridges one pixel wide (ridge skeleton)
   (7) MINUTIAE EXTRACTION: count ridge-pixel neighbours (1 = ending, 3 = bifurcation)
   (8) MINUTIAE DIRECTION: trace the ridge(s) a fixed distance
   (9) MINUTIAE FILTERING: remove spurious minutiae
        |
        v
   template (x, y, direction, type, quality of each minutia)  -> matching (B9)

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
B6  Singularity extraction and the Poincare index
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ASKED IN: 2022-02 Q10 ; 2025 Q9

ANSWER  [S p.91-93]
 POINCARE INDEX = the cumulative change of orientation along a CLOSED path in an
  orientation field; generally evaluated on the eight neighbours of a pixel (the 3x3 ring
  around it). WHERE USED: for the extraction of singular points (loops, deltas, whorls) in
  fingerprints - it is the L1 singularity-detection method.
 PROCEDURE (sketch: a ring of 8 small oriented bars around a pixel)
  1. Select an orientation for one element of the ring (e.g. the first one).
     (Use as input the smoothed orientation field of B5.)
  2. For each remaining element choose, among the equivalent orientations (orientation is
     only defined mod 180 deg), the one CLOSEST to the neighbouring element, i.e. each
     step difference lies in (-90, +90] degrees.
  3. Travel once around the closed curve and sum up the rotations.
  RESULT p: 0 deg = no singular point; 180 deg = loop (core); -180 deg = delta; 360 deg =
  whorl. [S p.92]  (Sign conventions differ between textbooks, the slide values above are
  the ones to give [W].)
 FULL METHOD (2022-02 Q10): orientation field estimation (gradient or FFT per block) ->
  smoothing of the field (cos 2theta, sin 2theta) so that noise does not create false
  points -> compute the Poincare index at every (block) position -> loop/delta/whorl by the
  value -> optionally determine the DIRECTION of each singularity by comparing the local
  orientation field with a reference orientation field of a loop/delta (the field around a
  true SP is a rotated version of the reference; alpha = rotation) - not strictly needed
  for classification, "but helps" -> use the SPs to classify the pattern (B2) and as
  alignment landmarks. Weakness: noisy/low-quality images give false singularities [W].

B7  Minutiae: what they are, types, extraction from a skeleton, direction
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ASKED IN: 2017 Q7 ; 2020 Q10 ; 2022-01 Q2 ; 2024-01 Q2 ; 2024-08 Q1 (minutiae part) ; N26

ANSWER  [S p.69-71, 95-99]
 WHAT: minutiae are the locations where a ridge EMERGES/ENDS, SPLITS or MERGES with another
  ridge (ridge characteristics). They are an abstract representation of the ridge
  skeleton, capture much of the discriminative information, are storage-efficient and
  reasonably robust to degradations; visible at ~500 ppi (L2 feature). Each minutia has
  a location (x, y), a DIRECTION and a TYPE.
 TYPES: ridge ENDING and ridge BIFURCATION (the two basic types). [X: finer types exist -
  dot/island, lake, spur, crossover - but templates normally store only these two.]
 EXTRACTION from a binarised, thinned (one-pixel-wide) image (sketch: 3x3 windows)
    ending           ridge              bifurcation
    . . .            . . X              X . X
    . X .            . X .              . X .
    . X .            X . .              . X .
   1 neighbour     2 neighbours        3 neighbours
  For every ridge pixel count the ridge pixels in its 8-neighbourhood:
   - exactly 1 ridge neighbour -> RIDGE ENDING (minutia)
   - exactly 2 ridge neighbours -> an ordinary ridge pixel, NO minutia
   - exactly 3 ridge neighbours -> RIDGE BIFURCATION (minutia)
  (Equivalent crossing-number form [X]: CN = 1/2 * sum |P_i - P_(i+1)| around the ring;
  CN = 1 ending, 2 ridge, 3 bifurcation, 4 crossing.)
 DIRECTION [S p.98]:
  - ENDING: trace the associated ridge to a fixed distance (say 10 pixels); the minutia
    direction is the angle of the line from the minutia to the traced point.
  - BIFURCATION: obtain three points by tracing the three ridges (branches) to a fixed
    distance; take the directions of the three branches and compute the MEAN of the two
    directions whose difference is the SMALLEST of the three. [!] (the solved sheet only
    says "mean of both")
  The direction convention should comply with ISO/IEC 19794-2:2011. [S p.99]
 Afterwards the spurious minutiae are filtered (B8).
 MAX NUMBER OF RIDGE NEIGHBOURS (oral O18.3): 3. After thinning every ridge is one pixel
  wide, so a ridge pixel has 2 neighbours on a plain ridge, 1 at an ending, and at a
  bifurcation the ridge splits into two branches -> 3 neighbours. More than 3 does not
  occur in a correctly thinned skeleton (a crossing would give 4, but is not a minutia type
  that is stored) [X: reasoning; the slide only gives 1 / 2 / 3].

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
B8  Removing spurious minutiae
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ASKED IN: note N2 ("How do we remove spurious minutiae points. Draw and describe.")

ANSWER  [S p.100, 73 ; W for the artefact types]
 Spurious (false) minutiae are produced by noise, creases, scars, dry/wet fingers and the
 thinning itself (breaks, spurs, holes, bridges). They would add wrong points to the
 template, so they are removed in the MINUTIAE FILTERING step.
 HEURISTIC METHODS (slide):
  1 remove minutiae located at the BOUNDARIES of the print / segmented area (border effect
    - the ridge is only cut by the image edge);
  2 remove minutiae that are CLOSE IN LOCATION and almost OPPOSITE IN DIRECTION (typical
    for a ridge break - two endings facing each other - and for a short ridge/spur/island);
  3 remove regions with TOO MANY minutiae in a small neighbourhood (noisy area).
 DUALITY OF MINUTIAE: process the POSITIVE (ridge) and the NEGATIVE (valley) image at
  the same time. A true ridge ending is a bifurcation of the valleys and vice versa, so a
  true minutia is found in both images and a spurious one (an artefact of binarisation/
  thinning) is not.
 SKETCH (ridges as lines):
   ridge break           spur / spike         hole / island        bridge
   ---->   <----         ----+----            ----+====+----       ----+----
   two facing endings    short branch         two bifurcations     ----+----
   -> remove both        -> remove the        close together       two bifurcations
                         ending+bifurc.       -> remove both       facing -> remove
 In NIST's MINDTCT the filtering is part of the detector, which also gives each remaining
  minutia a quality score. [S p.73]

B9  Alignment and matching of minutiae; parameters; non-linear deformation; pseudo-code
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ASKED IN: 2024-08 Q2 ; 2025 Q3

ANSWER  [S p.101-110]  Matching = 1. ALIGNMENT  2. CORRESPONDENCE (pairing)  3. SCORE
 1 ALIGNMENT (registration): determine the geometric transformation between the template
  and the query minutiae sets so that they lie in the same coordinate system.
  - A RIGID transformation is normally sufficient. PARAMETERS: translation (delta-x,
    delta-y) and rotation (delta-theta) - a "linear mapping"; (optionally scale as a 4th
    parameter - an extra dimension in the accumulator array).
  - Estimated with the generalized HOUGH TRANSFORM: every pair (template minutia, query
    minutia) votes for the transformation that would map one onto the other; the cell of
    the 3D accumulator (dx, dy, dtheta) that receives the MOST VOTES is the alignment.
    Useful modifications: scale dimension, weighted increment of the neighbouring cells,
    easy parallelisation, coarse-to-fine search. [p.103-106]
  - NON-LINEAR DEFORMATIONS (skin elasticity, pressure) are compensated for with a
    TOLERANCE "BOX" - the distance and angle tolerances in the pairing step; they are not
    modelled explicitly. [p.103] (Elastic models such as thin-plate splines also exist [X].)
  - If the overlap contains very few minutiae (fingermarks): use a minutia descriptor =
    a central minutia and its neighbourhood, pair the neighbours, and multiply the
    percentages of matched minutiae (e.g. 6/8 * 6/7 for a true match, 3/8 * 3/7 for a
    wrong one). [p.107]
 2 PAIRING: minutia a (template) corresponds to b (query) if their DISTANCE is within a
  predefined threshold (e.g. 15 pixels) AND the ANGLE between their directions is within
  another threshold (e.g. 20 degrees); each minutia may be matched to AT MOST ONE minutia
  of the other set (two flag arrays). [p.108]
 3 SCORE: number of paired minutiae; or percentage of matched minutiae in the overlap area;
  or number of paired minutiae divided by the average number of minutiae in both sets;
  then compare with a threshold -> genuine / impostor (two-class decision). [p.110]
 PSEUDO-CODE (from p.109, query set already aligned with the transformation parameters)
   input : template minutiae T = {(x_i, y_i, th_i)}, i = 1..M
           query minutiae   Q = {(x_j, y_j, th_j)}, j = 1..N   (after alignment)
           distance threshold t_d, rotation threshold t_r
   output: list of paired minutiae
   set flag arrays fT[1..M] = 0, fQ[1..N] = 0 ; count = 0 ; list = empty
   for i = 1 .. M
     for j = 1 .. N
       if fT[i] == 0 and fQ[j] == 0
          and distance(T_i, Q_j) < t_d
          and |rotation(T_i, Q_j)| < t_r then
            fT[i] = 1 ; fQ[j] = 1 ; count = count + 1 ; list[count] = (i, j)
   return list           ( score = count / ((M + N) / 2), compare with the threshold )
 EXAMPLES: Bozorth3 (NBIS, FBI): rotation/translation invariant; stores the relative
  positions of minutiae PAIRS, builds a compatibility table with distance/angle tolerances
  and clusters compatible pairs; higher score = more likely the same finger. Commercial
  VeriFinger: 31 matched minutiae -> score 614; 6 matched -> score 7. [p.75-76, 111]

B10  Fingerprint image quality: taxonomy, NFIQ / NFIQ 2, improving the quality
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ASKED IN: 2018 Q7 ; note N28

ANSWER  [S p.113-125]
 QUALITY FACTORS: image resolution (500 ppi; cheaper civilian sensors < 500 ppi), finger
  area (rolled vs swipe), clarity of the ridge pattern (dry finger -> broken ridges, moist
  finger -> joined ridges, creases, virtual ridges). Quality predicts recognition
  performance, so it is assessed to accept/reject/re-acquire a sample.
 TAXONOMY of quality-evaluation algorithms [?: no taxonomy figure could be recovered from
  the slide text; this is the version of the solved sheet, in line with how the literature
  groups the methods]:
        QUALITY EVALUATION
        /                    \
   LOCAL properties        GLOBAL properties
   (block-wise)            (whole image)
   - local ridge orientation   - orientation/direction-field continuity
   - Gabor filter responses    - uniformity of the ridge-frequency field
   - pixel intensity           - feature counting (e.g. number of minutiae)
   - power spectrum
        \                    /
         NFIQ combines local and global properties
 NFIQ (NIST Fingerprint Image Quality, 2004) [S p.118-119]: open source; the minutiae
  detector MINDTCT gives the minutiae points and a LOCAL quality map (each 8x8 block rated
  4 = best ... 0 = background; the block quality is derived from local image conditions -
  MINDTCT's low-contrast, low-flow and high-curvature maps [S p.74], and from ridge density
  and clarity, dominant block orientation, intensity); from the minutiae (each with its
  own quality) and the quality map an 11-value FEATURE VECTOR is built; a 3-layer NEURAL
  NETWORK classifies it into ONE global quality value 1 (excellent) ... 5 (poor). The value
  predicts recognition performance; registration with only class 1 and 2 quality is
  acceptable. (NFIQ thus combines LOCAL and GLOBAL properties.)
 NFIQ 2 (since 2010/2016; v2.3 Oct 2024) [S p.121-125]: removes NFIQ's weaknesses (single
  point of failure -> more feature extractors; more robust -> random forest; wider quality
  range); reference implementation of ISO/IEC 29794-4. Features (local with a sliding
  window, global, minutiae from FingerJetFX) -> fixed-length vector (69 values, histograms)
  -> RANDOM FOREST of 100 binary classifiers trained on class 0 = excellent (NFIQ 1) and
  class 1 = poor (NFIQ 5); quality score = number of trees voting for class 0 -> 0..100
  (higher = better). Best on contact optical sensors; questionable on contactless sensors
  and forensic fingermarks.
 IMPROVING THE QUALITY (note N28) [?: the wording is ambiguous; two readings]
  (a) improve the image = ENHANCEMENT: estimate the local ridge ORIENTATION and FREQUENCY
      (block FFT, smoothed, see B5), then filter each block with a Gabor-type filter
      tuned to that orientation/frequency [X] -> breaks are joined, merged ridges separated,
      noise suppressed -> better binarisation/thinning and fewer spurious minutiae. This
      is the one method to describe.
  (b) improve the sample = measure quality (NFIQ 2) and RE-ACQUIRE/reject poor ones, select
      the best samples at enrolment, merge several samples (multi-sample, E2). [S p.25, 350]

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
C2  How big is the iris code and why
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ASKED IN: 2018 Q8 ; 2024-08 Q3 ; note N3

ANSWER  [S p.156-160]
 The standard commercial iris code is 2048 bits (256 bytes), accompanied by a 2048-bit
 MASK.
 WHY: a 2D Gabor filter is applied at M x N grid points of the normalised iris image; each
 filter response is a point in the complex plane. It is demodulated, and the PHASE is
 quantised into 4 levels (one per quadrant), i.e. 2 BITS per sample point (the signs of the
 real and imaginary part). 2048 / 2 = 1024 sample points in the iris; 1024 = 8 radii x 128
 angular samples is a plausible sampling configuration. The mask has one bit per code bit
 and marks the bits that are unusable (eyelids, lashes, reflections).
 GABOR FILTER (oral O2.3, O11.5, O17.5, O22.5)  [S p.156-161]: a 2D Gabor filter = a complex
  sinusoidal wave under a 2D Gaussian window; its parameters are the CENTRE POSITION, the
  EFFECTIVE WIDTH and LENGTH of the Gaussian window (= the filter SIZE) and the WAVE
  DIRECTION with a spatial FREQUENCY. It is applied (convolution) at M x N grid points of the
  normalised iris image. WHY Gabor: possible relevance to human vision - Daugman: "different
  members of this optimal filter family are an excellent description of the 2D neural
  receptive fields found in the visual cortex" - and good theoretical properties (best joint
  localisation in space and frequency). WHAT WE GET OUT: one complex number per location
  (a point in the complex plane); DEMODULATION compresses the data: only the sign of the
  real and of the imaginary part (the PHASE quantised to the four quadrants) is kept = 2
  bits per location. Next step after normalisation: this Gabor filtering/phase quantisation
  gives the iris code (O27.3).
 Size trade-off [X]: 2048 bits are compact (fast XOR comparison, small template) and contain
 enough independent information: Daugman's impostor HD distribution is a binomial with p =
 0.5 and only ~249 independent degrees of freedom (the bits are correlated) [W], which
 already gives astronomically small false-match rates.

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
C3  Iris normalisation (rubber sheet) and the noise mask
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ASKED IN: 2020 Q9 ; 2022-01 Q5 ; 2025 Q5 ; note N30

ANSWER  [S p.154-155, 147-153]
 WHY NORMALISE: the iris image differs in size between images, subjects, sensors and
  imaging distances; the main concern is the pupil DILATION <-> CONTRACTION, which
  stretches or compresses the visible iris annulus. Without normalisation the same iris
  would give different codes. Normalisation maps the iris to a fixed-size frame.
 HOW: Daugman's RUBBER SHEET MODEL - the imaged iris (the annulus between the pupillary
  and the limbus boundary) is linearly stretched or compressed to a standard-size
  rectangular frame in polar coordinates: angle theta (0..2pi) along one axis, normalised
  radius r (0 at the pupil boundary .. 1 at the iris/sclera boundary) along the other.
  [X: x(r,theta) = (1-r) x_pupil(theta) + r x_limbus(theta), same for y; because the two
  circles are usually NOT concentric the mapping is done along each ray between the two
  boundary points.] The result is invariant to pupil size, centre position and image
  scale; a rotation of the eye only becomes a circular shift of the frame (handled at
  matching, C4).
 WHY A MASK: the NOISE MASK records the locations of undesired iris occlusions - eyelids,
  eyelashes, shadows, specular reflections. Those pixels carry no iris texture, so the
  corresponding code bits are masked out (marked 0, iris = 1) and are NOT used in the
  Hamming distance (only bits valid in both masks count). Otherwise noise would influence
  feature extraction/matching and raise the HD of genuine pairs. Segmentation that finds the
  boundaries: integro-differential operator (circular edge detector), eyelid parabola
  fitting, near-vertical edges for eyelashes. [S p.147-152]

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
C4  Iris matching: Hamming distance, genuine/impostor distributions, head tilt (rotation)
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ASKED IN: 2024-01 Q3 ; 2024-01 Q4

ANSWER 2024-01 Q3  [S p.162-164, 180]
 METRIC: the HAMMING DISTANCE (normalised or weighted), on codes and masks (C1/C3):
   HD = || (codeA XOR codeB) AND maskA AND maskB || / || maskA AND maskB ||
  = the fraction of bits that differ, counted only over bits that are iris (mask 1) in
  both. 0.0 = no difference, 1.0 = totally different, 0.5 = random agreement.
 DISTRIBUTIONS: the IMPOSTOR distribution is narrow and centred near 0.5 (bits of
  different irises agree by chance: binomial, slightly below 0.5, e.g. 0.4666 and 0.4708 in
  the slide's example); the GENUINE distribution lies at much lower HD (e.g. 0.2717 for two
  samples of the same eye), is broader with a longer tail towards higher HD (noise,
  occlusion, defocus, dilation, misalignment). So the impostor distribution has a higher HD
  than the genuine one, the two are well separated and a threshold gives very low error
  (prototype: HD threshold 0.427 -> FRR 11.58 % at FAR 0 %, CASIA v1; NIST ICE 2006:
  FNMR 1.1-1.4 % at FMR 0.1 %). [S p.179-182]
 TYPICAL VALUES (oral O12.3): genuine pairs ~0.1-0.3 (0.27 in the example above; means of
  0.19-0.26 for similar pupil sizes [S p.187]); impostor pairs ~0.45-0.5 (means 0.427-0.448
  in the slides' dilation experiment [S p.188]; theoretical 0.5); the threshold lies between
  (the prototype used 0.427). MATCHING PROCESS (oral O14.5): normalised masked HD for every
  circular shift of the code (see below), the minimum is kept and compared with the threshold.
ANSWER 2024-01 Q4 - HEAD TILT  [S p.165-166]
 - WHAT: we ROTATE the iris codes: to account for varying head tilt (rotation of the eye
   image) the code is circularly shifted in the angular direction.
 - HOW: the metric (HD) is computed for a RANGE of circular bit shifts (each angular
   sample = 2 bits, so a shift of one sample = 2 bits) to the left and to the right and the
   MINIMUM HD over all shifts is kept.
 - ADVANTAGE: corrects the AUTHENTIC distribution - genuine pairs that were rotated relative
   to each other no longer look different, so the genuine HDs move to lower values (fewer
   false non-matches).
 - DISADVANTAGE: increases the MATCHING TIME (HD computed once per shift) and CHANGES the
   IMPOSTOR distribution: taking the minimum over several comparisons pulls impostor HDs
   down and makes the peak narrower/higher (the figure shows the impostor peak moving from
   ~0.5 to ~0.46 with 3, 6, 9 shifts), so the distributions approach each other a little
   (slightly higher FMR for the same threshold).

C5  Pupil dilation, its influence, and iris aging
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ASKED IN: 2022-02 Q3 ; 2023 Q6 ; note N4 (dilation with age)

ANSWER  [S p.183-189, 202-208]
 WHY THE PUPIL SIZE VARIES (2023 Q6): change in ambient lighting, emotional state, health,
  medications, age, decision making (cognitive load).
 DAUGMAN'S STATEMENT: in his patent (US 5,291,560, 1994) he says that although the iris
  stretches and contracts, such distortions in the texture "can readily be reversed
  mathematically" to extract and encode an iris signature that remains the same over a wide
  range of dilations; the iris "proves to be essentially immutable over a person's life".
  (Weaver 2006 repeated: dilation does not adversely affect authentication.)
 HIS SOLUTION: the RUBBER SHEET model - linearly stretch/compress the iris to a standard
  size frame (normalisation, C3).
 THE EXPERIMENT (2022-02 Q3) [S p.185-189]: dilation information is available at
  segmentation but is discarded when going to the normalised image. Data: 18 subjects,
  632 iris images (LG 2200), 28 % of them taken with the lights off to induce dilation.
  Method: for each matched pair the DIFFERENCE IN DILATION RATIO was computed with a
  modified ICE baseline; assumption: the authentic and impostor distributions are
  conditioned by dilation.
  RESULTS (sketch two histograms of HD: genuine moving right, impostor fixed):
   - A larger difference in dilation SHIFTS THE MATCH (authentic) distribution to higher HD
     (worse) - there is a distinct curve for small, medium and large difference.
   - NO change of the impostor (non-match) distribution; the non-match MEAN even decreases
     somewhat for larger dilation (0.448 -> 0.427), "surprising but likely not a problem".
     (Oral O21.4: the examiner wanted to hear exactly this - the impostor MEAN decreases
     slightly - and was unhappy with "the whole distribution shifts a bit to the left".)
   - Larger pupil dilation increases the mean of the match distribution (similar-dilation
     pairs: mean 0.193 small pupils, 0.247 medium, 0.259 large pupils).
  CONCLUSION: when the difference in pupil dilation between two images is large, the
  FNMR INCREASES (FMR unchanged). NIST IREX I found similar results; the draft ISO iris
  format has a field for the pupil dilation ratio. Open questions: control acquisition,
  enrol multiple samples, better model of dilation.
 DILATION AND AGE (note N4) [S p.202-208]: ISO/IEC 19795-1 calls the increase of error
  rates with time "template aging". Medicine: pupil size decreases linearly with age at all
  illuminance levels (Winn et al.) -> a greater time lapse means on average a greater
  difference in dilation -> assumption: this degrades iris match scores. Experiments (43
  subjects, 86 irises, LG 4000, 2-year lapse, 1830 images; matchers IrisBEE and VeriEye):
  authentic distribution clearly DEGRADED, impostor distribution unchanged; 84/86 irises had
  a higher mean HD with a longer lapse; after 2 years FRR increased by 157-305 % (IrisBEE) and
  239-370 % (VeriEye). CONSEQUENCES: higher FNMR over time, possibly need to RE-ENROL or
  update the template (Flom and Safir's patent allows this; Daugman's patent says one
  enrolment lasts a lifetime - "which narrative won?"). The topic is CONTROVERSIAL: a NIST
  report on a large operational data set finds almost no iris aging.

C6  Fourier spectra of local iris regions: image quality (and the formula D)
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ASKED IN: 2020 Q8 ; 2022-01 Q10 ; 2024-08 Q4 ; 2025 Q8

ANSWER  [S p.167-178]
 IDEA: divide the segmented iris into local regions (e.g. 64 x 64 pixels) and analyse the
  2D Fourier spectrum of each; the distribution of power over low / middle / high spatial
  frequencies reveals the imperfection:
  - OUT-OF-FOCUS BLUR: the spectrum is largely dominated by LOW frequency components
    (defocus removes the highest frequencies, the low ones are practically unaffected).
  - MOTION BLUR: lacks middle and high frequency components; its frequency distribution
    is similar to that of an out-of-focus image.
  - OCCLUSION by eyelashes/eyelids: contains significant MIDDLE and HIGH frequency
    components (sharp edges).
  - A good, clearly focused, unoccluded texture has strong MIDDLE-frequency power.
 THE FORMULA (2022-01 Q10):   D = [ (F1 + F2 + F3) ; F2 / (F1 + F3) ]
   F_i = double integral over the band Omega_i of |F(u,v)| du dv, i = 1,2,3, with
   Omega_i = { (u,v) : f1_i < sqrt(u^2 + v^2) <= f2_i }  (a ring in the 2D spectrum;
   (f1_i, f2_i) = the radial frequency pair that are the extrema of the band),
   F(u,v) = 2D Fourier spectrum of the iris region; F1, F2, F3 = power of the LOW, MIDDLE
   and HIGH frequency components. It is the iris QUALITY DESCRIPTOR of the local region:
   - first component, the TOTAL spectrum power (F1+F2+F3), separates severely occluded
     images from high-quality images;
   - second component, the RATIO of the middle-frequency power to the other powers
     F2/(F1+F3), is high for a clearly focused image and low for out-of-focus or
     motion-blurred images.
   The mean of the resulting two local quality descriptors is taken as the quality measure
   of the whole iris image. Quality can also be used locally as a weight in matching
   (weighted Hamming distance, wavelet energy per concentric band, higher weights for the
   more stable inner bands). [S p.176-178]
 SHARPNESS/FOCUS EVALUATION IN DETAIL (oral O23.3) [S p.169-172]: sharpness = the normalised
  magnitude of the intensity gradient near the pupillary boundary, computed in a ROI:
   1 from the ROI compute the median of the pixels in the pupil region, Mp, and of the pixels
     in the iris region, Mi;
   2 for all ROI pixels with intensities in [Mp .. Mi] calculate the absolute values of their
     horizontal gradients;
   3 S = average of the top 20 values;
   4 sharpness = S / (Mi - Mp);
   5 if > 0.5 the image is considered well focused.
  Fourier alternative: defocus mainly removes the highest spatial frequencies (low ones are
  practically unaffected), so measure the TOTAL POWER at higher spatial frequencies - low
  power = defocused.
 FACTORS THAT INFLUENCE IRIS IMAGE QUALITY (oral O24.3) [S p.167-168]: occlusion (eyelids,
  lashes), defocus, motion blur, non-uniform illumination, low resolution / large imaging
  distance, pupil dilation, off-angle imaging, accessories (fake or printed contact lenses).
  Sensor/illumination point the examiner wanted: the illuminator's specular reflection should
  fall only on the PUPIL, not on the iris (reflections on the iris destroy texture; they are
  masked out, p.154). EFFECT ON THE DISTRIBUTIONS: poor quality raises the HD of genuine
  pairs (authentic distribution moves/gets a longer tail to the right -> FNMR up), the
  impostor distribution stays around 0.5 (same behaviour as for dilation and aging, C5).

C7  Printed (textured) contact lenses: detection and the role of the printing process
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ASKED IN: 2022-02 Q6

ANSWER  [S p.190-201]
 WHY IT MATTERS: clear lenses increase FNMR only slightly (minor issue); TEXTURED lenses
  are a security issue - they can be used to evade detection (an easy, intentional false
  non-match), to create a synthetic identity or (harder) to impersonate a target. So
  automatic detection is needed.
 DETECTION METHODS
  1 FOURIER analysis (Daugman): a printed iris is produced by a matrix (dot) printing
    process; this regular dot pattern leaves artefacts that are visible in the 2D Fourier
    spectrum of the iris image - 4 points of spurious energy (the central square of the
    spectrum is blanked so it does not dominate).
  2 TEXTURE analysis: iris edge sharpness, iris-texton features, co-occurrence-matrix
    features, LBP ... plus supervised classifiers; person-disjoint 10-fold cross-validation
    reports ~100 % correct classification of the lens types seen in training.
  3 A printed iris pattern does NOT undergo any distortion when the pupil changes in size
    (a real iris texture stretches/compresses with dilation) (Wei, ICPR'08).
 STILL IMAGE vs IMAGE SEQUENCE (oral O14.1): from ONE still image use Fourier analysis and
  texture analysis (LBP + classifier); from a SEQUENCE of images (pupil size changing, e.g.
  light flash) additionally check that the iris pattern does not deform with the pupil
  (method 3).
 LENSES THAT THE FOURIER METHOD MISSES (oral O9.4, O13.5, O28.2): the Fourier method works
  for "pixelated" lenses made by a dot-matrix printing process (regular dots -> spurious
  energy peaks). A lens whose pattern is not produced by a regular dot raster leaves no such
  peaks in the spectrum - then use texture-based detection (LBP, edge sharpness, texton and
  co-occurrence features + classifier) or the no-distortion-with-pupil-size test. [S p.198-200]
 DOES THE PRINTING PROCESS AFFECT THE DETECTION? Yes. The artefacts the detectors look for
  come from the printing process (dot-matrix pattern, printer resolution, ink/colour and
  the pattern design of each brand/type: e.g. for FreshLook alone there are 4 types with 3 to
  12 colours each). Different producers/printing processes give different spectra and
  textures: detection of textured lenses SEEN in training is a solved problem, detection of
  NEW textured lenses (open-set recognition) is NOT solved. The lens itself can also hide the
  real iris texture and cause a false non-match. [S p.201] [!] (the sheet only says "it can
  intentionally create a false non-match", which mixes up the lens effect with detection)

D1  Three levels of features for face recognition (and the IPD needed)

ASKED IN: 2022-01 Q1 ; 2025 Q4 ; note N27 ("L1 L2 L3 face ...")

ANSWER  [S p.219-222]
 (Anthropometric landmark measurements alone lack the distinctiveness needed for automated
  recognition, so features are grouped in three levels.)
 L1 - GROSS facial characteristics: the GEOMETRY of the face and the global SKIN COLOUR:
      a short round face vs an elongated thin face; faces with predominantly male vs female
      characteristics; faces of different races. Can be extracted even from LOW-resolution
      images: < 30 inter-pupillary distance (IPD).
 L2 - LOCALIZED face information: the STRUCTURE of the face components (e.g. eyes), the
      RELATIONSHIP between the facial components and the precise SHAPE of the face; this
      level gives ACCURATE face recognition; local-region characteristics can be represented
      with geometric or texture descriptors (e.g. Gabor, LBP, shape). 30 to 75 IPD.
 L3 - unstructured MICRO-level features: scars, freckles, skin discolouration, moles;
      useful e.g. for discriminating identical twins (the slide marks this with "?!").
      Needs the highest resolution - more than 75 IPD [?: the slides give no number for L3;
      ">75 IPD" is the value written in the solved sheet and is the logical continuation of
      "<30" and "30-75"; I could not confirm it from an independent source [W]].
 (IPD examples on the slides: IPD = 35, 12, 7 pixels in a 640 x 480 image, p.227.)

D2  Viola-Jones face detector: integral image, weak/strong classifier, cascade, features
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ASKED IN: 2017 Q9 ; 2020 Q6 ; 2022-01 Q7 ; 2023 Q7 ; 2024-01 Q5 ; 2024-08 Q5 ; N5

OVERVIEW  [S p.230-247]: VJ scans the input image with detection windows of different
 sizes and decides for each window whether it contains a face. In each window a classifier
 is applied to simple local features derived with rectangular (Haar-like) filters: two-,
 three- and four-rectangle filters. The feature value = the sum of the pixel intensities in
 the light rectangle(s) minus the sum in the dark one(s) (oral O24 note: the examiner
 corrected a student who spoke of "+1 for white, -1 for black, multiply with the pixels":
 say "the pixels under the white part are SUMMED, those under the black part are
 SUBTRACTED"; there are no negative weights/pixel values). When the combination of the filter
 responses exceeds a threshold, a face is detected. (Why light/dark regions: e.g. the eyes
 are darker than the cheeks and the bridge of the nose.)

INTEGRAL IMAGE (2017 Q9, 2022-01 Q7, 2024-01 Q5)  [S p.234-235]
 - WHAT: a new array in which the value at (x, y) is the SUM of ALL pixel intensities ABOVE
   and TO THE LEFT of (x, y) (inclusive) in the original image:
        S(x, y) = sum over x' <= x, y' <= y of I(x', y')
   Computed in ONE pass over the image [X]: S(x,y) = I(x,y) + S(x-1,y) + S(x,y-1) -
   S(x-1,y-1).
 - WHY: more than 180 000 different Haar-like features can be derived from a 24 x 24
   detection window, and every window of every size at every position needs them. With the
   integral image the SUM OF PIXELS IN ANY RECTANGLE needs only FOUR array accesses
   (S(D) + S(A) - S(B) - S(C) for the four corners), independent of the rectangle size.
   This is what makes the detector real-time. (Reduces the computational burden.)
 - WHERE/HOW: in the VJ face detector, computed once from the input image and then used to
   evaluate every Haar feature.

WEAK AND STRONG CLASSIFIER (2024-01 Q5)  [S p.236-242]
 - WEAK classifier: a classifier that only needs to EXCEED CHANCE performance (error < 0.5).
   Here: one Haar feature value f_j(w) of the window w, a polarity p_j (+/-1) and a
   threshold theta_j: h_j(w) = 1 if p_j f_j(w) < p_j theta_j else 0 [X]. (vertical or
   horizontal half-planes in feature space).
 - STRONG classifier: built by BOOSTING (AdaBoost variant). In each round the weak
   classifier with the lowest weighted training error is selected, the weights of the
   misclassified training examples are RAISED, and the next round concentrates on the hard
   examples. After T rounds (T << L, the total number of filters) the strong classifier is
   the WEIGHTED LINEAR COMBINATION of the T best weak classifiers; the weights
   alpha_t (= 1/2 ln((1-err)/err), e.g. err 0.3 -> 0.41, 0.21 -> 0.65, 0.14 -> 0.92) are
   proportional to the discriminative power, i.e. inversely related to the training error.
   A face is found if the weighted sum exceeds a threshold. AdaBoost therefore also does the
   FEATURE SELECTION.
   HOW THE WEIGHTS ARE SET (oral O28.3): all training examples start with equal weights;
   after each round the weights of the examples the chosen weak classifier got WRONG are
   raised (in the usual form w_i <- w_i * eps/(1-eps) for the correctly classified ones,
   then renormalised [X]) so the next weak classifier concentrates on the "hard" examples;
   "hardness" is captured by the weights attached to the training examples. [S p.238]
   BOOSTING in general = a supervised method that combines weak learners into a more
   accurate (strong) classifier, in multiple rounds. Detection stage: only the T selected
   feature values are computed per window, which makes the classification rapid.
   (Oral O24 remark: one examiner objected to "a strong classifier is built from several
   weak classifiers" - the slide wording is "weak learner = a classifier that only needs to
   exceed chance performance"; use the slide wording.)

CASCADE (2020 Q6, 2024-08 Q5, N5)  [S p.243-245]
 - WHY: even with boosting the whole image must still be scanned with windows of many sizes,
   and almost all windows are NON-faces. Spending the full classifier on each of them is
   wasteful.
 - HOW: a cascade of strong classifiers of increasing complexity. Classifiers with a simple,
   small set of filters are used in the EARLY stages to screen out a large number of
   non-face windows (reject as early as possible, while keeping nearly all faces), and more
   powerful classifiers are used only in the later stages on the few remaining difficult
   windows. A window is declared a face only when it passes ALL stages; one rejection ends
   the processing of that window. Connected stage by stage they act as one strong detector
   with a very low overall false-match rate.
 - NUMBERS (2017 Q9): with per-stage GMR = 0.99 and FMR = 0.3 the cascade of K stages has
   GMR = 0.99^K and FMR = 0.3^K. For K = 10: 0.99^10 = 0.904 ~ 0.9 and 0.3^10 = 5.9 x 10^-6
   ~ 6 x 10^-6 (order 10^-6). So 10 stages. [S p.244] (Strictly, FMR <= 10^-6 would need
   K = 12, with GMR 0.886; the slide's answer is 10.)
 - THE TWO MOST EFFECTIVE HAAR-LIKE FILTERS [S p.245]:
     (1) TWO-rectangle filter, dark rectangle on top, light below: the EYE region is
         typically darker than the CHEEK region;
     (2) THREE-rectangle filter, dark - light - dark horizontally: the BRIDGE OF THE NOSE is
         brighter than the two eye regions.
   Use them in the FIRST stage(s) of the cascade: together they can reject about 60 % of
   the non-faces [S p.245] while keeping the faces. [W: the original paper reports a 2-feature
   first classifier with ~100 % detection; the slide figure of ~60 % rejection is the one to
   quote, other sources say 40-50 % false positives.]

BRUTE-FORCE STEP AND INVERTED FACES (2023 Q7)  [S p.230-231, 243]
 - The brute-force part is the SCANNING of the input image with detection windows of
   different sizes (every position, every scale, no initial assumption about where or how
   big a face is; each window is classified). Speed-ups: integral image + cascade.
 - Inverted (also tilted, profile) faces are a problem because the Haar-like features are
   specific patterns of contrast between light and dark regions at fixed positions (eyes
   darker than cheeks, nose bridge brighter than eyes) and the classifier is TRAINED on
   upright frontal faces vs non-faces; turning the face upside down reverses the spatial
   arrangement, so the features do not respond (false negatives; the slide also shows false
   positives on cluttered background). Open problems: occlusion, pose, low resolution. [p.250]

D3  Face alignment: geometric warping vs landmark detection
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ASKED IN: 2020 Q5 ; 2022-01 Q4 ; 2024-01 Q6

ANSWER  [S p.251-262]
 ALIGNMENT BY GEOMETRIC WARPING: INPUT = the detected EYE POSITIONS plus output parameters:
  horizontal/vertical offset (the percentage of the image to keep next to the eyes) and
  the size of the output image. OUTPUT = a scaled, rotated and cropped image. Basic idea:
  rotate the face around one eye so that the eyes lie on a horizontal line at a fixed
  distance, then crop.
 PROBLEM: only the IN-PLANE rotation (plus scale/translation, i.e. a similarity transform)
  is addressed; it uses two points only, so it cannot handle out-of-plane pose (yaw/pitch),
  expression or face-shape differences.
 BETTER METHOD (more general alignment): LANDMARK DETECTION. Input: the facial image and
  the face position (from detection); output: the landmarks (fiducial points; e.g. 29, 68
  or 194 points depending on the database). With many landmarks we can ALIGN/FRONTALIZE the
  face, use pose-adaptive feature extractors, morph and synthesise. It is better because
  many points give a much richer description of the geometry (pose and shape), and the same
  idea is not limited to faces (the course also applies cascaded pose regression to ears).
  Approaches: active appearance models, constrained local models, parts-based methods,
  CASCADED REGRESSION (start from the mean shape; at every stage compute features at the
  current shape - pixel differences/comparisons, HOG - and regress the shape increment
  (least squares, trees, ridge, Gaussian process): S_(t+1) = S_t + R_t(F(I, S_t))).
 Mitigation of the geometric-warping problem (2020 Q5): use the landmark-detection method.
 CASCADES IN LANDMARK DETECTION (oral O12.4, O17.2, O21.5): the word "cascade" appears in
  TWO places in the course - (1) Viola-Jones: a cascade of CLASSIFIERS (D2); (2) landmark
  detection: a cascade of REGRESSION models. In (2) the training set (images with annotated
  landmarks) is used stage by stage: initial shape S1 = average of the reference annotations
  placed by the face detector; at stage t compute the feature vector x_i = F(I_i, S_t) at
  the current shape vertices, build the regressor matrix X_t of all n images, compute the
  required displacement DeltaS = S_ref - S_t for each image (output matrix), learn the
  regression model R_t (least squares, regression trees, ridge, Gaussian-process
  regression) and update S_(t+1) = S_t + R_t(X_t). Each stage refines the landmark
  positions. How many regressions are put together = the number of stages T, a design
  parameter [the slides do not fix it]. YES, the approach uses the TEXTURE of the face: the
  features at the current landmark positions are pixel differences, pixel comparisons or
  HOG. [S p.255-261]

D5  PCA, LDA, Eigenfaces, Fisherfaces
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ASKED IN: 2017 Q10 ; 2020 Q4 ; 2022-02 Q7 ; 2023 Q8 ; 2024-08 Q10

ANSWER  [S p.267-279]
 SUBSPACE IDEA: an A x B image is a point in R^(AxB); faces are highly correlated, so they
  are compressed to a low-dimensional subspace that captures the key appearance features;
  images are matched by comparing their vectors of weights.
 PCA (basis of EIGENFACES) - 2023 Q8 "basic concept, dimensionality reduction":
  - UNSUPERVISED (class labels are never used): learns the subspace that accounts for as
    much VARIABILITY (variance) of the training data as possible, via an eigenvalue
    decomposition of the covariance matrix.
  - Dimensionality reduction: the eigenvector with the largest eigenvalue is the principal
    axis (maximum variability). Keep only d' << d eigenvectors belonging to the d' LARGEST
    eigenvalues (sorted in descending order); they span a lower-dimensional subspace E'
    that still holds most of the variability, and the data are represented by d' weights
    instead of d pixels. Fewer eigenfaces = more information loss and less discrimination.
  - Matching: eigen-coefficients omega_G (gallery) and omega_P (probe); the Euclidean
    distance between them is the (dis)similarity.
 LDA (basis of FISHERFACES): SUPERVISED - uses the class labels; minimises the INTRA-class
  (within-class) variations and maximises the INTER-class (between-class) variations.
  Class means mu_j, scatter of class j S_j; within-class scatter S_W = sum_j S_j; between-
  class scatter S_B = sum_j N_j (mu_j - mu)(mu_j - mu)^T [X]. Maximise S_W^-1 S_B: solve
  the eigensystem S_W^-1 S_B E = lambda E; weights omega = E^T x; matching as in PCA.
 DIFFERENCE (2017 Q10, 2022-02 Q7, 2024-08 Q10):
  - EIGENFACES attempt to maximise the SCATTER of the training images in the face space
    (PCA); FISHERFACES attempt to maximise the BETWEEN-class scatter while MINIMISING the
    WITHIN-class scatter (LDA).
  - PCA: unsupervised, optimal for representation/reconstruction, its principal axis
    maximises the variance of the projected data (it may keep variation useless for
    classification, e.g. illumination); accuracy "cannot be expected to be very high".
    LDA: supervised, optimal for separation of classes - its axis is aligned so that the
    variance within each class is minimised and the separability of the classes maximised.
  [!] The solved sheet (2020 Q4) states it the wrong way round ("PCA maximises the variance
  inside the class and minimises between the classes").
 LDA IN DETAIL (oral O22.1, O9.5, O15.5, O20.3): LDA belongs to the APPEARANCE-BASED branch
  of the taxonomy (linear subspace). Procedure: (1) class means mu_j and the scatter S_j of
  every class; (2) within-class scatter S_W and between-class scatter S_B; (3) maximise
  S_B relative to S_W = solve the eigensystem S_W^-1 S_B E = lambda E; (4) projection weights
  omega = E^T x; (5) MATCHING: compute omega_G and omega_P of gallery and probe and compare
  with a distance (as for PCA). GETTING THE REPRESENTATION BACK: x ~ mu + sum_j omega_j e_j
  (a face is approximated as the mean plus a weighted sum of the eigen/Fisher faces; with
  fewer axes the reconstruction error grows). [S p.271-277]
 IF N < d (2020 Q4): S_W becomes SINGULAR. First apply PCA to reduce the dimensionality of
  the training data, then apply LDA to the transformed data of lower dimension (this
  combination is what is really called Fisherfaces).
 PSEUDO-CODE THAT GIVES THE EIGENFACES (2022-02 Q7)  [S p.270]
   input : training images x_1..x_N, each a d-dimensional column vector
           (the image pixels stacked); number d' of eigenfaces to keep
   output: mean face mu, eigenfaces E' = [e_1 .. e_d']
   1. mu = (1/N) * sum_{i=1..N} x_i                       (average face)
   2. X  = [ (x_1 - mu), (x_2 - mu), ..., (x_N - mu) ]    (centred data matrix, d x N)
   3. C  = X * X^T                                        (covariance matrix)
   4. solve C E = lambda E : eigenvalues and eigenvectors of C
   5. sort the eigenvectors by decreasing eigenvalue and keep the first d'  -> E'
   6. weights of any image x:  omega = E'^T (x - mu)      (coordinates in the face space)
   return mu, E'
  [X] when N < d it is cheaper to take the eigenvectors v of X^T X (N x N) and use X v.

D6  Gabor jets and Elastic Bunch Graph Matching
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ASKED IN: 2024-01 Q7

ANSWER  [S p.280-287]  (a MODEL-BASED method)
 JET = the set of GABOR COEFFICIENTS characterising the LOCAL TEXTURE around a fiducial
  point. One Gabor coefficient at a location = the response of convolving the image with a
  complex 2D Gabor filter centred at that location; by varying the filter's ORIENTATION and
  FREQUENCY we get a set of coefficients, the jet [typically 5 frequencies x 8
  orientations = 40 complex coefficients: X].
 IN WHICH SPACE / HOW BIG (oral O12.5): the Gabor coefficients are obtained in the IMAGE
  (spatial) domain by convolving the image with the complex Gabor filter centred at the
  fiducial point (a multiplication in the Fourier domain would be equivalent [X]). All jets
  have the SAME size - the same set of filter orientations and frequencies is used at every
  node, so every jet has the same number of complex coefficients (40 in the classic
  setting [X]) - which is what allows the jet-to-jet similarity to be computed.
 USED IN: ELASTIC BUNCH GRAPH MATCHING (EBGM) with the FACE BUNCH GRAPH (FBG). (Oral O24.1:
  "EFBG" - the examiner says only the abbreviation: it is Elastic Bunch Graph Matching /
  Face Bunch Graph; then say everything in this section.) A face is a
  labelled graph: each NODE = a fiducial point labelled with a jet; each EDGE labelled with
  the average distance between the two fiducial points.
 CONSTRUCTION of the FBG model (from training faces of a given pose): Stage 1 - manually
  mark the fiducial points and the graph structure in one (few) initial image(s), obtain the
  rest semi-automatically by comparing new images to model graphs using Gabor jets. Stage 2
  - stack the individual graphs; the set of jets of the same fiducial point is a BUNCH (a
  "local expert": it represents the local variation of that point in the population, e.g. an
  eye bunch has open, closed, male, female eyes).
 MATCHING (recognition with the FBG): 1 run elastic bunch graph matching on the probe:
  (a) take the mean model Gabor jets of each bunch and find the approximate face position at
  discrete locations; (b) refine the position by the similarity between a Gabor jet of the
  image and a bunch of model jets; (c) precisely locate the fiducial points by moving the
  nodes locally and relative to each other to optimise the graph similarity. 2 Compare with
  ALL model graphs: the similarity between the probe's image graph and a model graph = the
  AVERAGE similarity of the jets at corresponding fiducial points (works even with some
  missing nodes). 3 Take the BEST similarity score and apply a THRESHOLD to decide whether
  it is a valid match.

D7  HOG (sparse vs dense) and LBP / multiscale LBP for faces
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ASKED IN: 2022-02 Q4 ; 2024-01 Q10 ; 2025 Q6 ; 2023 Q9 (MLBP) ; note N21 (lbp)

HOG ANSWER  [S p.297-301]
 WHAT: Histogram of Oriented Gradients - an image DESCRIPTOR built from gradient
  orientations ("invariant to 2D rotation" on the slide; strictly, plain HOG tolerates only
  small in-plane rotations unless the dominant orientation is normalised [X]).
 SKETCH: take a patch of 8 x 8 pixels -> divide it into cells; for every cell compute the
  gradient (magnitude, orientation) of each pixel and a HISTOGRAM of the gradient
  orientations of that cell (e.g. 8-9 bins) = "HOG counts the occurrences of edge
  orientations"; the picture shows for every cell a star of bars giving the orientation
  strengths. (Optional block normalisation [X].)
 ARRANGEMENT: many descriptors are arranged either in local neighbourhoods around informative
  points (SPARSE) or across the entire image (DENSE).
  - SPARSE: descriptors only at key locations - FACIAL LANDMARKS found e.g. with an Active
    Appearance Model (slide: 24 x 24 and 64 x 64 HOG descriptors around the landmarks);
    fewer descriptors, so faster to compute/match, but needs a landmark detector (about
    +2.5 s per image) and its accuracy limits recognition [W].
  - DENSE: descriptors on a regular GRID of uniformly spaced cells covering the whole face
    image; no landmarks needed, tolerant to landmark errors (occlusion, pose,
    illumination) [W], more data per face; the slide: smaller descriptor sizes are better.
 WHERE USED: face recognition (FERET, MPIE, AR and Yale databases).
 MATCHING (2024-01 Q10, 2025 Q6): the (landmark-based) face image is divided into cells;
  for each cell the histogram of gradient directions is computed; the histograms of ALL
  cells are CONCATENATED into one feature vector; two faces are compared by a distance
  between these vectors (cosine distance works better); dimensionality reduction by PCA
  does not help but LDA (minimise intra-class, maximise inter-class variation) helps a lot;
  results of several descriptors can be combined at decision level.
 "HOG for face DETECTION" (2025 Q6): on the slides HOG is used for recognition; as a
  detector feature HOG + a linear classifier is slid over the image at several scales
  (Dalal-Triggs style) [X].

LBP / MLBP ANSWER (2023 Q9, N21)  [S p.302-307]
 LBP: a simple TEXTURE operator; features are obtained from a neighbourhood (3 x 3). The 8
  neighbours are compared with the CENTRE pixel (>= centre -> 1, else 0) and the result is
  an 8-bit BINARY code, converted to a decimal number by weighting each bit (2^k) and
  summing. Robust to monotonic illumination changes [X].
 MULTISCALE LBP (MLBP): two parameters - the NUMBER OF SAMPLING POINTS P and the RADIUS R
  (distance of the sampling points from the centre pixel); if a sampling point is off the
  pixel grid its intensity is obtained by BILINEAR INTERPOLATION. Small R (e.g. LBP 8,1)
  captures micro details, larger R (8,3 / 8,5) highlights macro features.
 FROM CODES TO A MATCH: after encoding every pixel the face is divided into several smaller
  WINDOWS; the HISTOGRAM of the codes is computed in each window (the slide: 8 bins for LBP
  and 2^P for MLBP; [X] a plain 8-neighbour LBP has 256 possible codes); the histograms of
  all windows are CONCATENATED and the vector NORMALISED = the global feature vector. Two
  faces are matched by the similarity/distance of these vectors (optionally with region
  weights: eyes/mouth weighted more, p.306).

ANSWER 2020 Q3 - discriminative information in each layer  [S p.328]
 Features were taken from each layer of an ImageNet-pretrained CNN and fed into a linear
 SVM or Softmax. HIGHER layers generally produce MORE DISCRIMINATIVE features: as the feature
 hierarchy becomes deeper, the network learns increasingly powerful features (low layers =
 generic edges/textures, upper layers = parts/objects [X]). Most probable explanation of why
 this works so well: LOTS OF DATA.
ANSWER 2018 Q9 - how people and CNNs recognise faces  [S p.218, 308, 328]
 The basic resemblance: both rely on HIGHER-LEVEL characteristics. Humans perceive a face
 from certain higher-level characteristics (holistic; they find it hard to detect or
 recognise INVERTED faces; prosopagnosia = loss of the ability to recognise faces only).
 A CNN is a hierarchy of feature extractors from simple to complex, and its higher
 layers carry the most discriminative (face-level) information. [!] the solved sheet adds
 "CNN perceives low and high level"; the part to stress is that the high-level
 representation does the recognition in both.
 ORAL VARIANT (O21.1: "the link between humans' difficulties in perceiving faces and the
 algorithms', because the recognition algorithm has the same problems"): the examiner wanted
 to hear VIOLA-JONES, even though the question said "recognition": humans find it difficult
 to detect or recognise INVERTED faces [S p.218], and the Viola-Jones detector has the same
 problem with inverted (and tilted) faces because it is trained on upright faces with
 Haar features of fixed light/dark layout [S p.230] (D2).
ANSWER N6 - removing deep layers (AlexNet, 8 layers, ImageNet, 18.2 % top-5 error)  [S p.323-327]
   removed                                  parameters dropped   performance drop
   top fully connected layer (layer 7)      ~16 million          only 1.1 %
   both FC layers (layers 6 and 7)          ~50 million          5.7 %
   upper feature-extractor layers 3 and 4   ~1 million           3.0 %
   layers 3, 4, 6 and 7 (only 4 layers)     most of the net      33.5 %
  CONCLUSION: the DEPTH of the network is key. Most parameters sit in the fully connected
  layers (removing them barely hurts), whereas the few parameters of the convolutional
  feature-extraction layers matter a lot (3.0 % for only ~1 M parameters) and removing
  several layers together hurts disproportionately (33.5 %, far more than the sum).
ANSWER 2025 Q10 - feature pyramids in RetinaFace  [S p.329-333]
 RetinaFace is a CNN face detector, a multi-task network: face classification (face / no
 face), bounding box, five facial landmarks, 3D face mesh - in one forward pass.
 FEATURE PYRAMID NETWORK (FPN):
  - BOTTOM-UP pathway: the backbone extracts high-level SEMANTIC features (resolution falls
    and semantics rise with depth);
  - TOP-DOWN pathway: ENHANCES the low-resolution (but semantic) features by up-sampling
    them and combining them with the bottom-up maps, so every pyramid level has both
    resolution and semantics;
  - detections (anchors of different sizes, then the context head modules) are made on
    every pyramid level => effective face detection across MULTIPLE SCALES.
  PURPOSE: detect SMALL faces in low-resolution regions and keep consistent accuracy across
  varying face sizes.

E4  Score-level fusion: combination rules, assumptions, verification vs identification
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ASKED IN: 2024-01 Q9

ANSWER  [S p.360-367]
 Score-level fusion (fusion at the measurement/confidence level): the match scores output
 by different matchers are consolidated. Challenges: scores may be distances or
 similarities, have different numerical ranges and different probability distributions.
 TRANSFORMATION-BASED score fusion: the scores are first normalised into a common domain
  (a monotonic transformation: min-max, z-score, median, tanh - the tanh/median variants are
  not sensitive to outliers), then COMBINATION RULES are applied: PRODUCT, SUM, MAX, MIN and
  MEDIAN rule ("sum of scores", "max score", "min score" after normalisation).
  - These rules approximate the Bayes decision rule (the likelihood-ratio approach would
    need M-dimensional density estimation, which is hard).
  - Product rule: a direct consequence of the assumptions, but sensitive to errors in the
    estimation of the posterior probabilities. Sum rule: generally more effective, esp.
    when the posterior estimates are unreliable (derived from the product rule assuming
    that the posteriors do not deviate much from the priors). Max, min and median rules are
    approximations of the product and sum rules. After normalisation the product rule is
    generally not applicable (scores have no probabilistic interpretation).
 TYPES OF SCORE FUSION (oral O16.1) [S p.361]: score-level fusion in verification mode
  learns a decision boundary between the genuine and the impostor class from a training set
  of score vectors; the taxonomy (the categorisation is not strict):
    - LIKELIHOOD-RATIO based fusion (Bayesian decision theory, Neyman-Pearson): parametric
      or non-parametric density estimation;
    - TRANSFORMATION-based fusion (classifier combination rules: sum, product, min, max);
    - OTHER CLASSIFICATION approaches: linear and non-linear functions (neural networks,
      SVM), stochastic methods (genetic algorithms), non-metric methods (decision trees,
      random forests).
 ASSUMPTIONS the rules must follow: (1) the match scores of different matchers are
  STATISTICALLY INDEPENDENT; (2) the PRIOR probabilities of the genuine and impostor classes
  are EQUAL.
 VERIFICATION vs IDENTIFICATION (key difference): in a verification system the score data
  is a VECTOR s = [s_1, ..., s_M] (one score per matcher for the claimed identity; the output
  is genuine/impostor); in an identification system it is an N x M MATRIX S = [s_n,m], s_n,m
  = the score of the m-th matcher for the n-th enrolled identity (N identities, M matchers).
  With minor modifications the rules of verification can be extended to identification.

E5  Rank-level fusion: highest rank, Borda count, logistic regression
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ASKED IN: 2022-02 Q8 ; 2024-08 Q8 ; notes N9

ANSWER  [S p.368-372]
 a) RANK-LEVEL FUSION: in the IDENTIFICATION mode the output of a matcher can be viewed as
  a RANKING of the enrolled identities. Rank fusion consolidates the ranks output by the
  individual subsystems into a CONSENSUS rank for each identity. Ranks reveal LESS
  information than match scores, but unlike scores the rankings of different matchers are
  comparable, so NO score NORMALISATION is needed. Let R = [r_n,m] be the rank matrix (r_n,m =
  rank of identity I_n from matcher m) and r^_n a statistic computed for user I_n; the user
  with the LOWEST r^ gets the highest consensus rank.
 b) BORDA COUNT METHOD: r^_n = sum over m of r_n,m  - the SUM of the ranks assigned by the
  individual matchers; a measure of the degree of agreement among the matchers. Assumptions:
  the ranks assigned by the matchers are statistically independent, and ALL matchers
  perform equally well.
 HIGHEST RANK METHOD: r^_n = min over m of r_n,m (each user gets the best rank any matcher
  gave); useful only when the number of users is large compared with the number of matchers;
  uses the strength of each matcher but gives ties (broken randomly).
 LOGISTIC REGRESSION METHOD: r^_n = sum over m of w_m r_n,m - a WEIGHTED sum of the ranks, a
  generalisation of Borda count; the weights are determined by logistic regression; useful
  when the matchers have significantly different accuracies; needs a training phase.
 WHERE USED (2022-02 Q8, N9): both are RANK-LEVEL fusion methods (identification mode).
 HOW TO GET BORDA FROM LOGISTIC REGRESSION: set ALL weights equal (w_m = 1, or 1/M): the
  weighted sum becomes the plain sum of ranks = Borda count (no training needed).
 EXAMPLE (two matchers, four users; the slide's illustration, weights 0.8 and 0.2):
   rank matcher 1: Alice 3, Bob 1, Charlie 4, Dick 2;  rank matcher 2: Alice 1, Bob 2,
   Charlie 3, Dick 4.
   Borda sum:      Alice 4, Bob 3, Charlie 7, Dick 6  -> consensus order Bob, Alice, Dick, Charlie
   Highest rank:   Alice 1, Bob 1, Charlie 3, Dick 2  -> Alice/Bob tie (broken randomly), Dick, Charlie
   Logistic regr.: 0.8*r1 + 0.2*r2 -> Alice 2.6, Bob 1.2, Charlie 3.8, Dick 2.4
                                                      -> Bob, Dick, Alice, Charlie
 Application in the course: fusing the sclera, periocular region and iris pipelines. [p.375]

ANSWER  [S p.373-374]
 DECISION-LEVEL (abstract-level) fusion: commercial off-the-shelf (COTS) matchers give

B11  Fingerprint image enhancement: what we enhance, the methods, Hong's algorithm
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ASKED IN (oral): O5.3, O15.3, O22.4, O24.2, O27.5, O29.3 ; note N28

WHAT AND WHY WE ENHANCE [S p.113-116, 95]: the clarity of the RIDGE STRUCTURE (the
 ridge/valley contrast). Poor clarity comes from a dry finger (many ridge breaks), a moist
 finger (adjacent ridges joined), creases, noise and low contrast. Enhancement reconnects
 broken ridges, separates merged ridges and suppresses noise so that binarisation, thinning
 and minutiae extraction work and fewer SPURIOUS minutiae appear (B8). It cannot invent
 structure where the print is destroyed ("unrecoverable" regions are masked out instead).
COMMON PRINCIPLE OF ALL METHODS [W]: (1) estimate the local ridge ORIENTATION and the local
 ridge FREQUENCY, (2) filter every neighbourhood with a filter tuned to them (contextual /
 oriented filtering) - the methods differ in how they do these two steps and in the domain
 (spatial or frequency).
METHODS the oral reports list: 1D smoothing, Fourier, "2SES", Hong.
 1 1D (directional) SMOOTHING [?]: smooth the grey levels along the local ridge direction
   with a one-dimensional low-pass kernel (and, optionally, band-pass across the ridges), so
   that noise and small breaks along a ridge are averaged out without blurring across the
   ridges. Needs a good orientation field. [description inferred; not on the slides]
 2 FOURIER method (works in the FREQUENCY space) - the part the slides do cover [S p.86-90]:
   locally the ridge pattern is approximated by a COSINE WAVE (amplitude, frequency,
   orientation); its 2D FFT is computed and the wave parameters are found from the MAXIMUM of
   the magnitude spectrum (the parameters we look for in the Fourier spectrum are the ridge
   FREQUENCY and ORIENTATION). Complications: the ridge pattern is not an exact cosine, so
   the FT gives a pair of blurred impulses -> first SMOOTH the magnitude spectrum with a
   low-pass filter, then detect the maximum; creases -> smooth the orientation field with the
   vector field V = (cos 2theta, sin 2theta) (orientation is only defined mod pi: the mean of
   1 and 179 degrees must be 0, not 90). For the enhancement itself: filter each (overlapping)
   block in the Fourier domain with a directional band-pass around that peak (e.g. multiply
   the block spectrum by its own magnitude, or a Gaussian band-pass), inverse-transform and
   recombine the blocks. This is the STFT-analysis scheme of Chikkerur et al. 2007, which
   estimates the region mask, orientation and frequency simultaneously [W, X].
 3 "2SES" [?]: I could not identify this acronym on the slides or online. From the reports it
   is the second method that works in the frequency space and searches the ridge FREQUENCY
   and ORIENTATION in the Fourier spectrum; the examiner asked for it in detail. If your
   lecture notes define it (the "2D enhancement scheme" of O15.3 may be the same thing), use
   their definition; otherwise present it as a two-stage Fourier scheme as in point 2:
   stage 1 = block-wise orientation/frequency/energy estimation and smoothing, stage 2 =
   directional filtering with those estimates [closest published match: two-stage STFT
   enhancement, W].
 4 HONG'S ALGORITHM (Hong, Wan, Jain, IEEE TPAMI 20(8):777-789, 1998) - spatial-domain,
   Gabor-filter based enhancement [W for the citation and the principle; the steps are from
   the paper as I know it, X]:
     a) NORMALISE the image to a prescribed mean and variance;
     b) estimate the block-wise ridge ORIENTATION field (gradient based) and smooth it;
     c) estimate the local ridge FREQUENCY (projection of the grey levels along the ridge
        direction -> distance between peaks), interpolate where it is unreliable;
     d) compute the REGION MASK: classify blocks as recoverable / unrecoverable;
     e) filter the recoverable region with an even-symmetric GABOR filter tuned to the local
        orientation and frequency (the Gaussian envelope is a few ridge periods wide) ->
        enhanced ridges; then binarise/thin.
   WEAKNESSES (O24.2) [W: "computationally inefficient"; "orientation cannot be estimated
   correctly in poor quality images"]: heavy computation (a separate Gabor kernel per pixel or
   block), depends fully on the orientation/frequency estimates (wrong estimates in noisy or
   high-curvature regions -> the filter creates false ridges), block artefacts, several
   hand-tuned parameters, unrecoverable regions are lost.
 PLACE IN THE COURSE: part of feature extraction (B5) before binarisation; improves the
  result of NFIQ-measured poor quality (B10); the orientation field it uses is the same one
  that feeds the singularity detection (B6).

B12  Henry classification system
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ASKED IN (oral): O17.4, O25.2, O27.4

ANSWER  [X; W for the origin and the 1024 groups; the slides only show the six pattern classes
 on p.66-67 and say "remember classes?" on p.94]
 The Henry system is the classical fingerprint CLASSIFICATION scheme (Edward Henry, 1896;
 extended by the FBI) used to file and search ten-print cards. It classifies the fingers of a
 card by PATTERN TYPE - arch/tented arch, loops (ulnar/radial = left/right loop), whorls (plus
 twin loops/composites) - i.e. the classes of B2, which are deduced from the number and
 position of singular points (loops, deltas; found by the Poincare index, B6). The primary
 classification puts the ten fingers into 32 x 32 = 1024 groups by giving every finger with a
 WHORL a weight (16, 16, 8, 8, 4, 4, 2, 2, 1, 1 for fingers 1-10) and forming a fraction
 (sum of the even-numbered fingers + 1) / (sum of the odd-numbered fingers + 1) [W/X].
 PURPOSE: partition the database so that a search compares the probe only with cards of the
 same class (indexing) - it narrows the search and never identifies by itself (it is not
 distinctive: ~65 % of prints are loops). Today the pattern class is used as an indexing aid
 in AFIS and as the L1 description (B1, B2); matching is done with minutiae (B9).

D10  SIFT for face recognition
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ASKED IN (oral): O5.1, O28.4

ANSWER  [S p.288-296]  SIFT = Scale Invariant Feature Transform; a TEXTURE-BASED method (gradient
 orientation branch of the taxonomy, D4). Motivation of texture-based recognition: raw pixel
 values are quite sensitive to ambient lighting and facial expression, so characterise an image
 by the distribution of LOCAL pixel values.
 TWO MAJOR STEPS: (1) KEY POINT extraction; (2) DESCRIPTOR calculation in a local neighbourhood
  of each key point. Orientation assignment: histogram (36 bins) of the local gradients at the
  selected scale; the canonical orientation (and magnitude) is assigned at the peak (if there
  are two major orientations, both are used).
 DESCRIPTOR: a histogram of gradient orientations within the local neighbourhood. The patch is
  divided into windows; each window has an orientation histogram with 8 bins; gradients are
  weighted by a Gaussian window. Slide's small example: 2 x 2 windows x 8 bins = 32-D vector; in
  practice 4 x 4 windows x 8 bins = 128-D FEATURE VECTOR per key point [S p.293; 128 = X].
 FOR FACES: the face image is divided into several patches, a SIFT descriptor is built from each
  patch and the final descriptor is the CONCATENATION of all of them.
 TWO TYPES (O28.4): (1) classic SIFT with key point detection; (2) DENSE SIFT - since the face
  images are roughly pre-aligned (e.g. by the eyes) the key point detection can be bypassed and
  the descriptor constructed directly from the entire face image.
 Problem: the number of key points can be large (hundreds) and finding the correspondences
  between the key points of two images is difficult. Tolerance against pose variations is the
  strength; assumption: pre-aligned faces.
 ADVANTAGES: locality, distinctiveness (matchable against a large database), quantity (many
  features even for small faces), efficiency (close to real time), extensibility.
 THREE MATCHING STRATEGIES: (1) minimum pair distance (distance between all descriptors of the
  two faces); (2) match eyes and mouth only (descriptors in these regions); (3) match on a
  regular grid (descriptors of sub-images). Results shown on the BANCA database (MPD, EM, RG).

D11  Perceptron, neural networks, CNN layers, ReLU, AlexNet
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ASKED IN (oral): O10.5, O11.1, O13.2, O17.3, O18.1, O20.1, O23.5

ANSWER  [S p.308-328, 335-339]
 DEEP LEARNING (p.308, 316): the representations of the earlier methods are not strongly
  invariant, so use a MULTI-LAYER neural network = a HIERARCHY of feature extractors from
  simple to complex. "Deep learning = just a multilayer artificial neural network"; a network is
  called DEEP when it has several (many) hidden layers.
 ARTIFICIAL NEURAL NETWORK (p.310): inspired by the human brain; artificial neurons connected by
  WEIGHTED links; the weights represent the strength of the connections; learning = adjusting
  the weights.
 PERCEPTRON (p.311-312; structure [X]): the simplest neuron. INPUT: the feature values x_1..x_n;
  each is multiplied by a weight w_i, the products are summed with a bias b (z = sum w_i x_i + b)
  and passed through an ACTIVATION function (in the classic perceptron a step function); OUTPUT: a
  single value, e.g. the class 0/1. A SINGLE-LAYER perceptron cannot model non-linear functions
  (only linearly separable classes); adding a HIDDEN layer with a non-linear activation function
  solves this (a multi-layer ANN with sets of adaptive weights learned during training).
 LAYERS of a feed-forward network (p.313): input layer (the input features), hidden layers (apply
  transformations with activation functions), output layer (final output); the data flow is
  one-directional (input -> hidden -> output).
 TRAINING (p.314-315, 321): 1 forward propagation (output from input and current weights), 2 loss
  calculation (cross-entropy in CNN classification), 3 BACKPROPAGATION (gradients for the weight
  adjustment - the efficient way to calculate gradients), 4 optimisation of the weights with
  (stochastic) gradient descent; key parameters: learning rate, batch size, momentum.
 CNN (p.316-320): convolution = image filtering (dot product of a filter with an image patch).
  Components: CONVOLUTIONAL layers (filters extract spatial features; hyper-parameters: filter
  size F, depth K, stride S, padding P; dependencies are local), the NON-LINEARITY - RECTIFIED
  LINEAR UNITS (ReLU, f(x) = max(0, x)): they simplify backpropagation, make learning faster and
  avoid saturation issues, POOLING layers (spatial pooling by max or sum over non-overlapping or
  overlapping regions, e.g. max pooling 2 x 2 with stride 2: reduces the feature-map size while
  keeping key information, gives invariance to small transformations and larger receptive
  fields), and FULLY CONNECTED layers (flatten and classify). FNN = static input, unidirectional;
  CNN = local connectivity, spatial grid-like data (images); RNN = temporal dependence (sequences).
 ALEXNET (p.323-328): 8 layers, trained on ImageNet, 18.2 % top-5 error; the first part is the
  FEATURE TRANSFORMATION (convolutional layers), the last part the DECISION MAKING (fully
  connected layers). What we showed with it: (1) features from each layer fed into a linear SVM
  / softmax -> HIGHER layers give MORE DISCRIMINATIVE features (most probably because of the
  lots of data); (2) removal of layers: depth is key (D8 table). Number of parameters: the
  FULLY CONNECTED layers hold most (~16 M in layer 7, ~50 M in layers 6+7), the convolutional
  layers 3 and 4 only ~1 M - "deeper = more parameters" in the sense that the last (dense)
  layers are the largest.
 DEEP FACE NETWORKS (p.335-339): CNNs (convolution, non-linear activation, weight sharing ->
  fewer parameters); DeepFace (2014, locally connected layers, LFW 97.35 %); modern ones are
  ResNet variants (residual learning prevents vanishing gradients; depth matters); losses that
  minimise intra-class variance and maximise inter-class separation: cross-entropy (softmax),
  triplet loss (margin between genuine and impostor pairs), angular margin loss; FaceNet 99.63 %
  (22 layers), DeepID2+ 99.47 % on LFW.

G1  Normalisation: what it is, what we can normalise, why
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ASKED IN (oral): O25.1

ANSWER  [S p.155, 251, 264-266, 356-366 ; X for the general definition]
 DEFINITION: normalisation brings samples (or scores) to a standard, comparable form, so that
 nuisance variation unrelated to identity is removed BEFORE features are extracted / compared.
 WHAT WE NORMALISE IN THE COURSE
  - IRIS: the variation in pupil size (dilation/contraction), imaging distance, sensor and
    subject -> Daugman's rubber sheet model maps the annulus to a fixed-size polar frame (C3);
    the Hamming distance is also "normalised" - divided by the number of valid (unmasked) bits,
    so it lies in [0, 1] (C4).
  - FACE: POSE - detection, landmarking and alignment/frontalisation (geometric warping or
    landmark detection, D3); ILLUMINATION - active methods (3D acquisition, near-IR, thermal
    images) or passive methods: photometric normalisation (histogram normalisation, gamma
    intensity correction, local normalisation), illumination-variation modelling (linear
    subspaces, illumination cone, generalized photometric stereo) and illumination-invariant
    features (direction of gradient, shape from shading, quotient image, eigen-phase, LBP).
    Also scale: crop and resize by the eye distance (IPD).
  - FINGERPRINT: the orientation field is smoothed with the doubled-angle vector field (B5);
    in Hong's enhancement the image is normalised to a prescribed mean/variance (B11) [X].
  - MATCH SCORES in score-level fusion: the scores of different matchers (distance or
    similarity, different ranges and distributions) are mapped to a common domain with a
    monotonic function - min-max, z-score, median, tanh - before the sum/max/min rules (E4);
    rank fusion needs no normalisation (E5).
 WHY: to make the same identity look the same (reduce intra-class variation), make
 measurements from different images, sensors and subjects comparable, and make combination
 across matchers meaningful.
